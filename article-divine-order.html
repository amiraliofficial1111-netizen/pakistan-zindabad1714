What “Divine Constitutional Order” Means in This Book

The manuscript does not begin with America. It begins with a claim about God, and it asks the reader to treat that claim as a constitution.

Mark H Steffen · 18 August 2026 · 6 minute read

Readers who open The Constitution of God expecting a commentary on 1787 will meet a different first move. Chapters 1 through 3 are all, in their titles, about an original constitution that belongs to God: “The Original Constitution of God,” “The Constitutional Concept of God for the New World,” and “God’s Original Constitution.” The repetition is the point. I am not using “constitution” as a decoration for a sermon. I am asking the reader to treat divine order as something with articles: a structure that grants, limits, and directs.

On this site that structure is named in three words the book keeps in front of the reader: absolute faith, absolute love, and absolute obedience. They are not offered as a mood. They are the commitments the manuscript is organized around. A constitution, in this usage, is not a mood either. It is the prior arrangement inside which later history — sin, lineage, nations — has to be read.

Why the civic question has to wait

That is why the early chapters matter even for someone whose real question is civic. If the order is not established first, the later claim that America’s founding ideals can echo it has nothing to echo. The chapter list calls chapters 1 through 5 the Foundations path: constitutional origin, the fall, and the early framework, including “The Origin of Sin” and “Adam and Eve’s Life After the Garden.” The fall is inside the constitution’s story, not an interruption that makes the constitution irrelevant. Something given is damaged. The rest of the book is about how what was given is addressed.

A practical way to read these chapters is to keep a two-column note. In one column, write the claim about God. In the other, write what that claim would require of a person. The promise of the book, as I have put it on this site, is a constitutional and theological reflection on creation, purpose, and destiny. The first column without the second becomes abstraction. The second without the first becomes advice with no ground.

Do not skip the opening

Teachers sometimes skip openings. Here that skip is expensive. Chapter 12, “The American Constitution,” and chapter 14, “The Purpose of Life and the Desire of America,” are intelligible as sequels. Read as openers, they look like a thesis the book has not earned yet. The essay on the Second Adam picks up the hinge in the middle. If foundations are the ground, chapter 6 is the turn.

If you are deciding whether this volume is yours, use that test. If you want a book that will keep Scripture and a national ideal in one argument, and you are willing to start before the nation appears, start at chapter 1. If your question is already about Christ and restoration, the reader quiz may send you to chapter 6 instead — not because the opening does not matter, but because a reader who begins at the hinge can still walk back. The chapters are numbered so that walk is obvious.

I ask for one posture, and it is printed on the home page: read with an open mind. For these opening chapters that means letting the word “constitution” stay theological until the book itself carries it into civic life. Do not translate it too early. The translation is a later chapter. It is not the definition.
