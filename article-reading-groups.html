How to Read These Fourteen Chapters with a Group

A fourteen-chapter book can be rushed until no one remembers it, or wandered until no one knows the argument. The table of contents is already the cure.

Mark H Steffen · 6 October 2026 · 7 minute read

Use the groups this site publishes. Foundations are chapters 1 through 5: original constitution, the concept of God for the new world, the constitution again, the origin of sin, and life after the garden. Restoration is chapters 6 through 10: the Second Adam, the third pair, the lineage chain, the birth named in chapter 9, and the claim that Jesus must return. Nation and purpose are chapters 11 through 14: the makeup of creation, the American constitution, God’s desire, and the purpose of life joined to the desire of America.

Three meetings can cover the spine if time is short: one sitting for each path, with one chapter read in the room and the others assigned. Fourteen meetings can cover the book if the group wants the real thing, one chapter a week. That fits a season from autumn to spring, or a disciplined summer. Do not invent a fourth path. The table of contents is the curriculum.

Four questions, every meeting

Each meeting needs the same four questions, or the hour will follow whoever speaks first.

First: what does this chapter claim about God? Second: what does it claim about human beings — Adam, Christ, a lineage, a reader? Third: what, if anything, does it claim about a people or a nation? Fourth: which of the book’s three commitments — absolute faith, absolute love, absolute obedience — did this chapter put weight on?

Write the answers in sentences, not in topics. “It says that…” is a better note than “lineage.” The Resources page suggests the same discipline in plainer clothes: read one chapter, capture the key claims in plain language, then cross-check themes before the discussion. That order matters. Cross-checking first produces a report on other books. The manuscript should speak before the shelf does.

Read the chapter before the title

A leader should say, at the first meeting, that the titles in the middle of the book are specific and will not be softened in advance. “Third Adam,” “lineage chain,” and “Jesus must return” are the subjects, not the group’s metaphors. Read the chapter before debating the title. The study review makes the same caution: these public pages describe the arc more fully than they quote the prose, so the assigned chapter, not the path guide, is the text.

Who the group is for is already named on the home page. Individual readers who want a pace. Teachers and pastors who need an opening that can be taught. People forming a circle who want a sequence rather than a topic list. Interviews and events are a different door. They belong on the contact form, not in the living room.

If the group cannot agree where to start, do not vote by volume. Take the reader quiz separately, then compare results. A room split between chapter 1, chapter 6, and chapter 11 has just discovered the book’s three doors. Begin at chapter 1 anyway if you intend to finish — the numbers run forward for a reason — and let the people who were sent to chapter 6 or 11 present, when you arrive, why that door was theirs. You will get a better discussion than if you had pretended everyone came with the same question.

Close each meeting by naming the next chapter’s title out loud. The titles are doing argumentative work. Hearing “Jesus Must Return” a week before you read it changes how you sit. Hearing “The Purpose of Life and the Desire of America” at the end of chapter 13 tells the group what the book thinks it has been building. Let it.
