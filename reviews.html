How the Book Is Built

Three reviews from this site’s editorial desk. They assess the argument Mark H Steffen has made public: the chapter architecture, the civic claim, and the book’s fitness for a group. They are not reprints from another magazine.

Structure

One Argument in Fourteen Chapters

Official site editorial desk · 22 October 2026

Scope: the 2026 edition’s chapter titles, the three reading paths, and the author’s framing on this site. Interior pages are not quoted here beyond lines the author has already published.

Mark H Steffen’s The Constitution of God proposes a single constitutional reading of divine order, human restoration, and national purpose. The edition organizes that proposal as fourteen named chapters, three public paths, and a set of commitments printed with the work: absolute faith, absolute love, and absolute obedience.

Arc
Fourteen chapters, three doors, one volume
Best for
Readers who want Scripture and civic life in one frame
Read first
Chapter 1, unless the quiz sends you to a hinge

The table of contents does not behave like a list of topics. The first three titles all concern an original constitution that belongs to God. That repetition is structural. “Constitution” is not being used as a decoration for a sermon. It is the name of an order that later history — sin, lineage, a nation — has to be read inside.

The site divides the fourteen chapters without breaking the book into volumes. Foundations are chapters 1 through 5, from the original constitution through Adam and Eve after the garden. Restoration is chapters 6 through 10, from Jesus as the Second Adam to the claim that Jesus must return. Nation and purpose are chapters 11 through 14, ending at “The Purpose of Life and the Desire of America.” A reader can see the joints. A teacher can assign them.

What the public pages do not yet do is stand in for the prose. The author’s request, printed on the home page, is to read with an open mind. That request cuts both ways. Open enough to let the word constitution stay theological until the book carries it into civic life. Alert enough to notice that a path guide is a map, not the chapter. Study leaders should read the assigned chapter before they teach from the titles.

Judged by its published architecture, the manuscript is unusually explicit about its own sequence. Many books in this neighborhood offer a mood and a set of asides. This one offers an order of argument. That is the feature worth a reader’s time, and it is also the standard the book should be held to: does chapter 14 actually conclude chapter 1, or only share a cover with it?

The reader quiz is the practical consequence of that architecture. People do not arrive with the same question. The book still has one spine. Naming the door is not the same as offering four books.

Verdict. For a reader who wants one constitutional frame rather than a shelf of disconnected claims, this is the volume to open. Start at chapter 1 if you intend to finish. Start at the hinge only if you know you will walk back.

Civic argument

An Echo, Not a Slogan

Official site editorial desk · 22 October 2026

Scope: chapters 11 through 14 as titled and described on the official site, read against the opening claim of a divine constitution.

The closing movement does something specific and risky. It treats America’s founding ideals as a civic echo of a prior theological order. An echo is not an identity. The thing that sounds is not the thing that first spoke. That distinction is the difference between a theological argument and a campaign.

Arc
Creation, then the nation, then desire
Best for
Readers uneasy with the split between faith and citizenship
Read first
Chapter 11, not a headline about the flag

Read the last four titles in a row: “The Makeup of Creation,” “The American Constitution,” “God’s Desire,” “The Purpose of Life and the Desire of America.” The thesis of the ending is hard to miss. A particular nation’s constitution is named inside a structure that belongs to creation. Desire — God’s, then America’s — is discussed in the same register as a person’s purpose.

The home page calls this the convergence of Heaven’s purpose with America’s founding ideals, and it uses the careful word. Echo. Chapter 12 can be a civic echo only if chapters 1 through 3 have already said what the divine constitution is. A reader who drops in at chapter 12 because the title matches a debate already underway has entered mid-sentence. The site allows that entrance. It should also tell the truth about it, and the journal essay does.

The condition is plain. The book, as Steffen describes the project, is for people who take Scripture and the American experiment with equal seriousness. Equal does not mean Scripture is trimmed until it fits a civics class, and it does not mean the civics disappear into a private piety. Readers looking for a partisan briefing are in the wrong room. The manuscript is theological first. The nation arrives late because the argument thinks it must.

“The Makeup of Creation” sitting just before the American chapter is good table-of-contents judgment. A discussion that starts at symbols will get symbols. A discussion that starts at chapter 11 has a chance of asking what kind of world a constitution is for. Then the titles turn to desire, which is a moral word. Applied to a nation, it asks what the nation is for, not only what it is permitted to do.

A legal constitution is largely permissions and restraints. A constitution in the sense introduced at the front of this book is also a statement of purpose. The last chapter tries to hold those senses together. Whether the prose earns the try is a question only the chapter can answer. The contents commit the author to the try. That commitment is already more interesting than a slogan.

Verdict. Strongest for readers who refuse to keep Sunday and citizenship in separate boxes, and who will accept a prior theological order as the thing America is said to echo. Hold the book to the distinction it publishes. Echo is not identity.

For groups

A Book That Can Be Taught

Official site editorial desk · 22 October 2026

Scope: the chapter grid, the published paths, the resources page, and what a leader can honestly assign from this site today.

Fourteen chapters can die in a group by being rushed or by being replaced with a cloud of themes. The Constitution of God is built against both habits. The chapters are numbered, named, and already grouped. A leader who follows that grouping has a syllabus. A leader who improves on it usually has a different book.

Arc
Three paths, or one chapter a week
Best for
Churches, seminars, and readers who will finish
Read first
The assigned chapter, before the path guide

Three meetings can cover the spine if time is short: one sitting for each path, one chapter read in the room, the others assigned. Fourteen meetings can cover the book if the group wants the real thing. That pace fits a season. It does not fit a single weekend, and the contents do not pretend otherwise.

The resources page already suggests the right order of work. Read one chapter. Capture the key claims in plain language. Cross-check themes before the discussion, not instead of the chapter. Groups that reverse this produce a conversation about other books. The manuscript should speak first.

A reservation belongs in the review, because a useful review is not a brochure. The middle titles are specific — “The Appearance of the Third Adam and Eve,” “The Lineage Chain,” “Jesus Must Return” — and the public pages describe that arc more fully than they quote the prose. The group essay says the same thing in the form of advice: read the chapter before debating the title. The path cards are not a substitute text. Additional study materials are described as a destination on the resources page; until a downloadable guide is posted, leaders should write their questions from the chapter in front of them.

The three commitments — faith, love, obedience — give a group a shared vocabulary without giving it a script. Asking which commitment a chapter puts weight on is a better prompt than “what did we feel.” The book, judged from its own front matter on this site, is a constitutional and theological reflection. Feelings may follow. They are not the assignment.

Who the room is for is already named: individual readers who want a pace, teachers and pastors who need an opening that can be taught, and circles that want a sequence rather than a topic list. Media inquiries are a different door. They belong on the contact form.

Verdict. Recommended as the common text for a three-arc course or a fourteen-week reading. Begin at chapter 1 if the group intends to finish. Let people who were sent to chapter 6 or chapter 11 say, when you arrive, why that door was theirs. You will get a better hour than if everyone had pretended to bring the same question.
